# Trading Journal Template for Prop Firm Traders

A trading journal should tell you what you traded, when you traded it, which setup you saw, whether you followed your rules, and what you will check next time. It should not become a long diary that you stop using after three days.

This template uses clear labels such as **XAUUSD**, **London session**, **liquidity sweep**, and **breakout and retest**, so you can group comparable trades instead of writing vague notes like “gold trade” or “my usual setup.” You can copy it into a spreadsheet, note-taking app, or notebook.

> This template is for education and self-review. It does not provide trading signals, recommend how much to risk, calculate account eligibility, or guarantee better results.

## How to use this template without making journaling a second job

Start with the smallest version that answers your review questions. For many traders, that means five parts:

1. Context: what, when, and which setup.
2. Plan: what had to be true before entry.
3. Execution: what actually happened.
4. Compliance: whether the trade followed the setup and account rules.
5. Review: one useful lesson or follow-up question.

Complete the factual fields soon after the trade, while the details are still available. Save deeper interpretation for a separate [trade review](/glossary/trade-review). This keeps the journal entry short and reduces the temptation to rewrite the story after seeing the result.

The wider [prop firm trading journal](/blogs/prop-firm-trading-journal) guide explains how individual entries fit into a broader review system. This page focuses only on the reusable template.

## What each section helps you review

### Trade and account context

Context lets you group comparable trades later. Date, session, market, account, and setup name help you check whether a pattern belongs to one setup or appears across different conditions.

Account or program belongs in the record because prop-firm rules can vary. Enter the exact account you were using instead of assuming one firm's limits apply everywhere.

If you are reviewing trades from India, record IST when session timing matters. Keep trading values and P&L examples in USD with the `$` symbol; the Indian audience setting does not convert forex trading figures to INR.

### Plan before the trade

This section preserves what you intended before the outcome was known. Record the setup requirement, invalidation condition, planned exit method, and the risk unit you already use.

The template does not supply a risk percentage. Risk choices depend on the trader's plan, circumstances, and current account rules. The journal's job is to record the choice clearly enough to review later.

### Execution

Execution fields compare the plan with what happened. A simple yes-or-no field can make a vague note more useful:

- Did the setup qualify?
- Did position size match the plan?
- Was the stop or exit changed?
- Was an extra trade added without a plan?

These questions assess the process. A profitable result does not turn a rule break into compliant execution, and a losing result does not automatically mean the process was poor.

### Result and process review

Record the result, but keep it beside process fields rather than using it as the only score. Add costs or fees if they matter to your records. Then write what was done well, what should change, and one short lesson.

The lesson should describe something you can check again. “Be more disciplined” is too broad. “Wait for every item on my setup checklist before entry” is specific enough to review.

### Behaviour tags

Tags turn repeated descriptions into groups that can be counted and reviewed. Use only tags you understand and apply consistently. “Rushed,” “distracted,” and “plan followed” are more useful than a long list that changes every week.

Tags are observations, not diagnoses. They should help you notice when a behaviour appears, not explain your mental health or predict the next trade.

## A clear XAUUSD example

Imagine a trader who planned one XAUUSD setup during the London session. Their written setup was a **liquidity sweep of the Asian session range followed by breakout confirmation**. This is a fictional journal entry that demonstrates clear naming; it is not a market forecast or a setup recommendation.

| Field | Example entry |
|---|---|
| Account or program | Demo evaluation account |
| Market or instrument | XAUUSD |
| Session | London session |
| Setup name | Asian range liquidity sweep + breakout confirmation |
| Chart timeframe used for review | 5-minute chart |
| Reason the setup qualified | Price moved beyond the trader's marked Asian range and returned; the trader then waited for the confirmation required by their written plan |
| Relevant account rule | Current account limits checked before the session |
| Pre-trade emotion | Calm while waiting, then impatient after the initial move |
| Plan followed? | Partly |
| What changed? | Entry was taken before the breakout candle had closed |
| Result | Small gain |
| Process review | The gain does not remove the early-entry mistake |
| One-sentence lesson | Wait for the breakout candle to close before marking this setup as confirmed |

The useful sequence is **Asian session range → London liquidity sweep → planned breakout confirmation → actual execution → review**. The result remains separate from the process review so a positive outcome cannot hide a repeatable mistake.

## Use stable names for sessions and setups

Use **Asian session**, **London session**, or **New York session**, then record the review timezone separately. For an Indian reader, that may be IST. Keeping both fields avoids confusion when daylight-saving changes affect international session times.

Give each setup one stable name. Examples include **breakout**, **liquidity sweep**, **breakout and retest**, and **range reversal**. These are categories for review, not instructions to enter a trade. Define the checklist behind each label in your rulebook and keep the wording stable so the same setup does not appear as several unrelated spreadsheet groups.

## How to review several journal entries

An individual entry captures evidence. The review turns several entries into a useful question.

At the end of the week, filter or scan entries by:

- Setup name
- Session
- Plan followed: yes, partly, or no
- Account-rule compliance
- Behaviour tag
- Mistake or lesson

Then choose one repeated pattern to examine. Do not change five things at once. ## Common trading-journal template mistakes

### Recording only P&L

P&L tells you the outcome. It does not show whether the setup qualified, the plan changed, or an account rule was tested. Keep the result, but record enough process information to explain it.

### Writing vague notes

“Bad trade” and “felt emotional” are difficult to review. Name the observable event: entered before confirmation, changed the stop, added an unplanned trade, or skipped a checklist item.

### Changing the template every few days

Frequent changes make entries harder to compare. Use one core template for a meaningful review period. Add or remove a field only when you can explain which question the change will answer.

### Journaling only difficult trades

Selective records distort the sample. A template works best when the same core fields are completed for the trades covered by your review plan, including trades that ended well.

### Collecting entries without reviewing them

A full spreadsheet is not automatically a useful journal. Set a repeatable review time and use the entries to answer one practical question about process or rule adherence.

## Spreadsheet, notebook, or journal app?

Use the format you can complete consistently.

- A spreadsheet is helpful for filtering, formulas, and repeated fields.
- A notebook is simple and flexible but harder to search across many entries.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a trading journal template include?

At minimum, include trade context, the plan before entry, what happened during execution, the result, whether the plan and relevant account rules were followed, and one review note. Add fields only when they answer a real review question.

### Can I use this trading journal template in Excel or Google Sheets?

Yes. Use one column per structured field and one row per trade. Longer notes can stay in dedicated columns or a linked note. Keep the layout simple enough to complete after each trade.

### Do I need screenshots in every journal entry?

No universal rule requires a screenshot. Use one when it preserves context that your written fields cannot capture efficiently. Apply the same screenshot rule consistently so entries remain comparable.

### How often should I review my trading journal?

Choose a schedule you can follow consistently. Individual entries can be checked after the trade, while a weekly review can look for repeated patterns. A monthly review can compare several weeks. The right schedule depends on your process and number of recorded trades.

### Is this template specific to one prop firm?

No. It intentionally avoids firm-specific limits. Enter the exact rules for your current firm, program, and account from their current official source. Recheck them when the account or program changes.
