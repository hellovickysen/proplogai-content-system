# Direct Trader Teaching Standard

## Write to one trader

Write as if you are sitting beside one trader and helping them understand what happened.

- Use `you` and `your` when it sounds natural.
- Recognise real moments: you may feel an urge to enter, recover a loss, chase a move, or keep trading after your planned session.
- Use calm prompts such as `pause`, `check your plan`, `write down what changed`, and `wait for the setup you already defined`.
- Do not shame the reader, diagnose their emotions, or speak like a lecturer.
- Do not hide the answer behind formal phrases such as `the trader should consider` when `you can check` is clearer.

The article should sound like a useful conversation with a trader. It should not sound like a research paper, a sales page, or text written to impress a search engine.

## Use very simple English

- Prefer common words: `change` instead of `deviation`, `check` instead of `evaluate`, and `reason` instead of `rationale` when the meaning remains accurate.
- Keep sentences short. Keep one main idea in each paragraph.
- Explain the idea before adding detail.
- Use a concrete instrument, session, setup, and journal note when an example helps.
- Write so a motivated non-trader can follow the main lesson.

## Explain trading words

Avoid jargon when a plain phrase works. When a trading term is necessary:

1. explain it in plain English at first useful mention;
2. link that mention to its approved glossary page when one exists; and
3. use the same term consistently after it has been explained.

Do not place several unexplained terms in one sentence. If no reviewed glossary page exists, explain the term in the article and record the missing glossary destination as a content gap.

## Teach the decision in small steps

Build from the moment the trader recognises:

1. what they feel or notice;
2. what fact they should check;
3. what the written plan says;
4. what they can record; and
5. what they can review later.

Use a fictional example to show the steps. Keep process quality separate from whether the trade won or lost.

## Choose the simplest useful visual

An interactive element is optional. Use it only when the reader learns more by changing an input, selecting a step, or comparing states.

A handwritten learning-note visual is often a better choice for a simple concept. It may use paper texture, hand-drawn arrows, highlighted phrases, sticky notes, a simple chart, and short labels. It must feel human and easy to follow, while keeping every trading fact accurate.

For a process or chart explanation, prefer a progressive sequence:

1. show the starting chart or situation;
2. add the first marked level or decision;
3. add the next action or result;
4. show the completed annotated view last.

Each panel should teach one new point. Do not begin with one crowded image that makes the reader decode every label at once. A final complete view may combine the earlier steps as a recap.

The same handwritten direction may be used for a cover when it remains clear at thumbnail size. Covers and in-article images must still be delivered as WebP, checked on desktop and mobile, and free from generated text errors, false chart logic, signals, forecasts, or profit promises.

## Final reader test

Before human review, ask:

- Does this sound like we are talking directly to a trader?
- Can a non-trader explain the main idea after one read?
- Is every necessary trading term explained or linked?
- Does each visual make the lesson easier?
- Could any sentence be simpler without losing accuracy?
- Is the practical next step about checking and recording the reader's own process rather than following our trade instruction?
